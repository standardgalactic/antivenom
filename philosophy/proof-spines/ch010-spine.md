# Spine: ch010 — Reconstruction as an Inverse Problem

**Volume:** I: The Epistemology of Suspicion
**Part:** II. Events, Records, and Reconstructions
**Status:** spine

## Claim
Reconstruction is an inverse problem in which one seeks \(\widehat H=\arg\max_{H\in\mathcal H}P(H\mid E)\), but the result remains model-dependent because the posterior is shaped by priors, likelihoods, and the hypothesis space itself.

## Depends on

- ch007 — transformation histories constrain what a surviving trace can legitimately be taken to show.

## Load-bearing steps
1. The chapter frames reconstruction as the inverse problem \(\widehat H=\arg\max_{H\in\mathcal H}P(H\mid E)\), where surviving evidence must be mapped back to an unobserved history.
2. It emphasizes that the posterior depends on four formal choices: the hypothesis space \(\mathcal H\), the prior over candidate histories, the likelihood model, and the handling of unknown alternatives.
3. That dependence shows why Bayesian language relocates judgment into model construction rather than dissolving judgment into pure calculation.
4. The chapter therefore grounds rigor in explicit comparison among rival histories and explicit disclosure of the model that makes one reconstruction look best-supported.

## External cases / technical analogues

| Case | Role | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Forensic reconstruction from partial physical traces | Illustrative | Captures the inverse-problem structure of inferring a past process from surviving outputs. |
| Historical reconstruction from damaged archives | Illustrative | Shows how compression, loss, and provenance jointly constrain competing past narratives. |

## Objections this chapter must survive
- If every reconstruction is model-dependent and rival histories always remain live, then objective history collapses into underdetermined storytelling with no principled way to rank accounts.
- Response: model-dependence is not arbitrariness; corroboration, regularities, provenance, and explicit rival comparison yield graded but real differences in epistemic warrant.
