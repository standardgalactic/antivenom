## Claim
Privacy is control over boundary-crossing conditions across collection, inference, combination, retention, retrieval, interpretation, and action, not merely secrecy about hidden facts.

## Depends on
- ch023 — making everything visible does not itself produce knowledge, so total exposure cannot be justified as an epistemic cure-all.
- ch024–ch027 — overload, scarce attention, flooding, and manufactured salience show that more visibility can disable rather than secure public understanding.
- ch028 — public knowledge depends on organized, contestable collective investigation rather than continuous unstructured inspection of persons.

## Load-bearing steps
1. The chapter defines privacy by control over how information crosses contextual boundaries, extending beyond disclosure to collection, inference, combination, retention, retrieval, interpretation, and action.
2. It distinguishes informational, decisional, spatial, relational, inferential, and temporal privacy as different boundary structures rather than different kinds of hidden content.
3. This boundary account explains why ``nothing to hide'' is a category mistake: the issue is not possession of secrets but the conditions under which traces may be extracted and reused.
4. Agency, rehearsal, and role differentiation depend on selective disclosure across those boundaries, so total legibility would damage capacities that responsible inquiry itself presupposes.
5. Privacy is therefore a positive epistemic and political capacity, not a mere residue left over after legitimate scrutiny has finished.

## Objections this chapter must survive
- Objection: privacy rhetoric mostly protects wrongdoing and entrenched power, so treating privacy as a positive good simply obstructs accountability.
- Response: the chapter distinguishes privacy for persons and role-bound relationships from secrecy for coercive institutions; it defends bounded spaces that make agency and truth-telling possible, not blanket opacity for power.
