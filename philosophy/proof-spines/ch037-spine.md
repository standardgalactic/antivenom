# Spine: ch037 — Admissibility

**Volume:** IV: Law as an Epistemic Machine
**Part:** VII. Evidence Before Action
**Status:** spine

## Claim
Admissibility is the context-sensitive operator $\mathsf A_c:\mathcal E\to\{0,1,\bot\}$ that sorts available material into admitted, refused, or foundation-pending evidence so only institutionally usable inputs may bear legal weight.

## Depends on
- ch035 (The Architecture of Proof) — the chapter assumes evidence has a procedural role inside a proof structure, not merely a truth-like relation to the facts.
- ch036 (Burdens and Standards of Proof) — once legal actors know what must be proved and to what threshold, they still need rules about what kinds of inputs may count toward meeting that burden.

## Load-bearing steps
1. The chapter defines admissibility over the full material space $\mathcal E$, making clear that ``available information and ``usable evidence are not the same set.
2. The operator $\mathsf A_c:\mathcal E\to\{0,1,\bot\}$ is context-sensitive: procedural context $c$ determines whether a given item is admitted, refused, or left unresolved pending added foundation.
3. The operator's criteria are the outline's five named disciplines---relevance, provenance, reliability, prejudice, and contestability---rather than mere existence or loose factual bearing.
4. The special value $\bot$ is load-bearing because some materials are neither accepted nor rejected outright; they remain in a foundation-seeking state until the proponent repairs the missing link.
5. Admissibility therefore functions as a type system that keeps institutionally corrupting inputs from doing evidentiary work they are not procedurally qualified to do.

## External cases / technical analogues

| Case | Role | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *Crawford v. Washington* (2004) | Illustrative | Sharp example of excluding testimonial hearsay because untested statements cannot simply be counted as if adversarially vetted. |
| *Old Chief v. United States* (1997) | Illustrative | Useful for the point that some evidence is excluded or limited not because it lacks relevance, but because its prejudicial force distorts warranted inference. |

## Objections this chapter must survive
- **Objection:** Excluding relevant evidence in the name of admissibility sacrifices truth to technicality; if a tribunal is mature enough, it should hear everything and decide what weight to give it.
- **Response:** That assumes tribunals can reliably neutralize every source of opacity and prejudice after exposure; admissibility starts from the more realistic premise that some inputs predictably damage reasoning once admitted.
