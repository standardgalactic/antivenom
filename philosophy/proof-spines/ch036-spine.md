# Spine: ch036 — Burdens and Standards of Proof

**Volume:** IV: Law as an Epistemic Machine
**Part:** VII. Evidence Before Action
**Status:** spine

## Claim
Burdens and standards of proof are action-threshold rules, approximated by $A\text{ permitted if }P(H\mid E)>\theta_A$, with $\theta_A$ rising as the magnitude, irreversibility, and asymmetry of the contemplated intervention increase.

## Depends on
- ch035 (The Architecture of Proof) — the chapter presupposes that proof is a public structure assigning roles and sequence, then specifies how that structure allocates uncertainty.

## Load-bearing steps
1. The chapter aligns legal standards---probable cause, reasonable suspicion, preponderance, clear and convincing evidence, and beyond a reasonable doubt---with different permissions to act rather than with one undifferentiated notion of proof.
2. The threshold schema $A\text{ permitted if }P(H\mid E)>\theta_A$ clarifies what all these standards share: some action $A$ becomes licit only after evidence pushes support for $H$ past a context-sensitive cutoff.
3. That schema is only approximate; the doctrinal standards are not secretly exact numbers, but the model captures their function as public thresholds for institutional action.
4. The decisive variables are the magnitude, irreversibility, and asymmetry of the contemplated intervention, so higher-stakes coercion should correspond to a higher $\theta_A$.
5. Burdens of proof then specify who must do the threshold-crossing work, while standards specify how far uncertainty must be moved before force is allowed.

## External cases / technical analogues

| Case | Role | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *In re Winship* (1970) | Illustrative | Canonical statement that criminal conviction requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt because the cost of convicting the innocent is exceptionally high. |
| *Addington v. Texas* (1979) | Illustrative | Shows an intermediate threshold for civil commitment, making visible that the law calibrates standards to stakes rather than using one universal truth-meter. |

## Objections this chapter must survive
- **Objection:** These standards are verbal fog: jurors and even judges cannot reliably distinguish phrases like "clear and convincing" from "beyond a reasonable doubt," so the supposed allocation of error is mostly aspirational.
- **Response:** Even imperfectly applied thresholds still matter because they specify who loses when proof is ambiguous, shape jury instructions and review, and publicly encode which errors a polity treats as least tolerable.
