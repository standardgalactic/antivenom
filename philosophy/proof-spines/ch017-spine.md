## Claim
Evidence is governed by event time \(t_e\), recording time \(t_r\), and recognition time \(t_s\); because \(t_e\leq t_r\ll t_s\) is common, warrant depends on the latency of evidence as much as on the evidence itself. The chapter also fixes a four-part delay taxonomy — procedural, capacity, strategic, pathological — so that only procedural delay is credited with epistemic restraint for the rest of the Part.

## Load-bearing steps
1. Because evidence is distributed across event time \(t_e\), recording time \(t_r\), and recognition time \(t_s\), inquiry must model not just what happened but when a trace was created, stored, and understood.
2. Because the common pattern \(t_e\leq t_r\ll t_s\) creates latency of evidence, a detail can be present in the record long before anyone realizes its significance.
3. Because traces also decay, corroborate, or change meaning over time, warrant is stage-relative: the same proposition can be weakly supported early and strongly supported later without the world changing.
4. Because institutions often must act before \(t_s\) catches up, good procedure distinguishes provisional from mature judgment and treats the clock as part of the proof rather than as an external inconvenience.
5. Because not every source of delay does the same evidentiary work, the chapter separates procedural delay (evidence maturing between \(t_r\) and \(t_s\)) from capacity delay (insufficient throughput), strategic delay (an actor benefiting from dissipated attention or witnesses), and pathological delay (indifference or dysfunction) — only the first licenses the claim that waiting protects judgment.

## Objections this chapter must survive
- **Objection:** Truth is timeless, so tying belief to "when" just confuses epistemic weakness with reality itself. **Response:** The proposition does not change with time, but warrant does because the evidentiary ecology matures, decays, and recontextualizes traces.
