# Spine: ch061 — Strategic Transparency

**Volume:** VI: Adversaries, Platforms, and Automated Judgment
**Part:** XI. Untrustworthy Actors
**Status:** spine

## Claim
Transparency is strategic rather than self-validating, because visible compliance, auditor-flooding, unusable disclosure, and category-shifting can simulate openness without enabling audit.

## Depends on
- ch059 — adversarial agency must be modeled in bounded, strategic terms rather than assuming that more visible behavior is automatically more innocent.
- ch060 — correlated capture can survive behind apparently open and plural institutions, so openness itself must be evaluated for whether it preserves real independence.

## Load-bearing steps
1. Define transparency as strategic by listing the outline's four exploit modes: staged visible compliance, auditor-flooding, unusable disclosure, and migration of consequential activity into unmonitored categories.
2. Distinguish visibility from auditability, since each exploit mode can increase what is publicly seen while decreasing what can actually be checked.
3. Treat category boundaries and monitoring blind spots as part of the transparency regime itself, because actors can win by moving action into what the system ignores.
4. Conclude that publicity is not a passive condition but a contested field whose design must be judged by whether it enables review rather than theater.

## External cases / technical analogues

| Case | Role | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Spam and abuse filters after rule disclosure | Illustrative | shows how full publication of thresholds can improve adversarial gaming |
| Police or corporate "full document dumps" with strategic redaction | Illustrative | illustrates disclosure that performs openness while obscuring what matters |

## Objections this chapter must survive
- Once institutions are permitted to call secrecy "strategic opacity," they will hide bias, incompetence, and abuse behind exactly that language.
- Response: opacity is justified here only when paired with accountable audit, constrained scope, and review by actors not controlled by the decision-maker.
