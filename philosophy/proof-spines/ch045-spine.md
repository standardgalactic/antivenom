# Spine: ch045 — Presumption of Innocence

**Volume:** IV: Law as an Epistemic Machine
**Part:** VIII. Legal Friction as Freedom
**Status:** spine

## Claim
The presumption of innocence is a normatively weighted decision rule: institutions move from innocence default to conviction only when the posterior odds exceed the error-cost ratio $\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}}$, so accusation never counts as its own confirmation.

## Depends on
- ch036 (Burdens and Standards of Proof) — the presumption gives concrete direction to the chapter's allocation question by fixing who must move uncertainty and who receives the benefit of unresolved ambiguity.
- ch043 (Refusal as a Positive Operation) — the presumption is sustained only if the accused need not supply the very evidence that would dislodge it.
- ch044 (Due Process as Constrained Updating) — the chapter takes over the claim that institutional revision must be proportionate to tested evidentiary reach, then specifies that accusation alone may not shift the baseline from which that revision begins.

## Load-bearing steps
1. The chapter begins by separating the presumption of innocence from any empirical claim about the frequency of actual innocence among the accused.
2. Its formal device is the odds test $\frac{P(H_1\mid E)}{P(H_0\mid E)} > \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}}$, where the conviction threshold depends on the cost ratio between false conviction and false acquittal.
3. That ratio is normative rather than merely statistical: it encodes which error the institution is less willing to tolerate once coercive judgment becomes possible.
4. Because $\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}}$ sits on the right-hand side, accusation alone cannot shift the baseline; tested evidence must do enough work to overcome a default that protects against wrongful conviction.
5. The presumption thus functions as an allocation rule for uncertainty, ensuring that unresolved doubt remains with the state rather than being silently transferred to the accused.

## External cases / technical analogues

| Case | Role | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *Coffin v. United States* (1895) | Illustrative | Canonical articulation that the presumption is a legal principle governing adjudication, not merely a moral platitude about nice treatment. |
| Pretrial detention and perp-walk controversies | Illustrative | Useful for showing how quickly institutions and publics treat accusation as self-confirming unless the presumption actively constrains rhetoric and treatment. |

## Objections this chapter must survive
- **Objection:** The presumption of innocence is socially naive: many accused people are in fact guilty, and treating them as presumptively innocent can endanger the public.
- **Response:** The presumption is not an empirical forecast about who committed the act; it is an operating rule for institutions whose coercive errors are especially hard to reverse once accusation has already set closure in motion.
