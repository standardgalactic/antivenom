# Spine: ch089 — Scientific Misconduct

**Volume:** VIII: Applications and Case Structures
**Part:** XV. Domains of Difficult Judgment
**Status:** spine

## Claim
Scientific misconduct must be handled by separating anomaly, suspicion, investigation, finding, and sanction across authorship disputes, data irregularities, peer review, and replication failure, because trust in results depends on answerable production processes rather than scandal reflexes.

## Depends on
- ch088 (Employment and Institutional Reputation) — institutions often manage scandal and liability more readily than they expose the evidentiary basis of their judgments.
- ch038 (Cross-Examination as Adversarial Testing) — claims become stronger when authorized opposition can probe inconsistency, incentive, and selective presentation.
- ch040 (Appeal and Reversible Judgment) — correction mechanisms such as retraction, replication, and reopening are part of inquiry rather than embarrassments to it.
- ch074 (Update Budgets) — a single striking result should move communal belief only in proportion to provenance, independence, and replication.
- ch081 (Constraint Relaxation) — temporary loosening of deference can surface suppressed concerns, but it is dangerous unless paired with answerable safeguards.

## Load-bearing steps
1. Authorship disputes, suspicious images, missing raw data, peer-review failure, and replication breakdown are heterogeneous signals, so institutions must resist collapsing them immediately into either proved fraud or harmless anomaly.
2. Distinguishing anomaly, suspicion, investigation, finding, and sanction is essential because each stage authorizes a different level of publicity, inference, and institutional response.
3. Data access, image screening, protected whistleblowing, and replication are the mechanisms by which concern about a result becomes concern about its production process in a testable way.
4. Institutional conflicts of interest matter throughout, since universities, journals, funders, and laboratories may prefer reputational containment to evidentiary clarity.
5. Science sustains trust only when it can investigate the provenance of its own claims without confusing preliminary irregularity with final guilt or, conversely, prestige with immunity.

## External cases / technical analogues

| Case | Role | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Jan Hendrik Schön and Diederik Stapel fabrication cases | Load-bearing | Clear examples where spectacular results, prestige, and weak scrutiny delayed recognition that the evidentiary process itself was compromised. |
| Routine screening for duplicated or manipulated scientific images | Illustrative | A concrete practice for turning diffuse suspicion about data irregularities into reviewable evidence. |
| Replication-failure disputes in psychology and biomedicine | Illustrative | Shows why failed replication is often a trigger for investigation rather than itself a final finding of misconduct. |

## Objections this chapter must survive
- **Objection:** Constant suspicion of laboratories and authors will chill innovation and replace trust with bureaucratic policing. **Response:** The chapter targets provenance, documentation, and replicability—not the blanket presumption that every novel result is bad faith.
