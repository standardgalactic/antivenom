## Claim
Procedure loses legitimacy when evidentiary rigor, delay, and counsel become asymmetrically accessible resources, letting wealthy or insulated actors convert due process into indefinite protection while poorer parties are processed through rapid coercion.

## Depends on
- ch083–ch090 (Part XV collectively) — the application chapters showed that disciplined suspicion needs staged thresholds, tested evidentiary reach, and institutions that match judgment to decision type across criminal, public, wartime, medical, familial, employment, scientific, and historical cases.
- ch075 (Privacy–Publicity Duality) — informational environments shape whether reliable evidence can even surface before procedure begins.
- ch076 (The Freedom Region) — freedom requires constraints on excessive legibility and brittle response, but those constraints can be captured by the strong.
- ch082 (Reknotting After Error) — a repaired institutional order must remember how failure arose instead of reenacting it under cleaner formal language.

## Load-bearing steps
1. Due process is not evenly usable: money, counsel, status, and insulation from retaliation determine who can demand exhaustive proof, procedural repetition, and extended delay.
2. The same legal order that subjects poorer actors to plea pressure, pretrial detention, or summary discipline can let wealthy actors stretch identical formal rights into practical nonaccountability.
3. Procedural rigor therefore cannot be evaluated abstractly; it must be judged against the distribution of time, expertise, and survival costs required to invoke it.
4. When asymmetries are large enough, burdens of proof, disclosure fights, confidentiality rules, and hearing rights no longer function mainly as truth-seeking safeguards but as tools for power preservation.
5. The theory must accordingly refuse to romanticize existing legal systems: friction is defensible only when access to it is not itself stratified by wealth, rank, and dependence.

## External cases / technical analogues

| Case | Role | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Wealthy defendants or institutions using exhaustive motion practice and appeals to defer accountability | Load-bearing | Directly captures the chapter's core asymmetry: rigor as a resource for delay rather than a neutral protection. |
| Cash-bail and plea-bargain pressure on poor defendants | Load-bearing | Shows the converse condition in which nominally identical due-process rights are practically inaccessible under coercive time pressure. |
| Forced-arbitration or internal grievance systems with major counsel asymmetries | Illustrative | Extends the point beyond criminal law into administrative and employment procedure. |

## Objections this chapter must survive
- **Objection / failure mode:** If friction, burden, and review can be captured by the already powerful, then the book's own proceduralism may just rationalize domination. **Response:** Partly conceded—this chapter narrows the theory by making equalizable access and anti-capture design conditions of legitimate procedure rather than optional supplements.
