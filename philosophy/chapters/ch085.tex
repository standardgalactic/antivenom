\chapter{War, Intelligence, and Atrocity Evidence}
\label{ch:ch085}

War pushes the theory into environments where evidence is urgent, partial, and strategically corrupted. This chapter expands the analysis to damaged archives, propaganda, classified sources, satellite imagery, chain-of-command inference, and delayed forensic access. It asks how institutions should reason when early reports may be morally urgent enough to guide warning or protection, yet still evidentially incomplete for public attribution, sanction, or prosecution.

The point is not that wartime judgment must wait for impossible purity, but that different decisions authorize different evidentiary travel. Classified intercepts, refugee testimony, crater analysis, command patterns, and later exhumations do not arrive in one unified form, and they rarely mature at the same speed. The chapter therefore treats atrocity assessment as a problem of tiered judgment under hostile conditions rather than as a choice between gullibility and paralysis.
