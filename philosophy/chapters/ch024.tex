\chapter{The Evidentiary Haystack}
\label{ch:ch024}

Suppose \(N\) records contain only \(k\) relevant items. Under random inspection, expected search cost scales like \(N/k\). Mere disclosure can therefore bury evidence under mass even before anyone argues about what it means. Salience tools do not dissolve the problem, because the features used to rank evidence already encode a theory of relevance; they help only by presuming, and thereby partly fixing in advance, what counts as worth finding.

This yields the paradox of search: \emph{to find the evidence that changes the theory, one often needs a theory capable of recognizing the evidence}. The chapter treats the haystack not as a natural fact about abundance but as an institutional design problem. Duties to disclose are incomplete unless they are joined to preservation, indexing, provenance, and forms of organization that make unexpected evidence retrievable rather than merely available.
